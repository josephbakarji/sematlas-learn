Days before his fatal duel in 1804, Hamilton left a list of his papers that claimed the twelve. Madison later said they were his. Historians argued for 160 years.

In 1963, Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace settled it with counting and Bayes' rule. Today we redo their work, and it is the whole of generative learning.

Can you tell them apart?The opening of a paper by Hamilton or by Madison. Guess.

Both write long, careful, eighteenth-century sentences about the same constitution. Topic gives little away, and neither does style, read by eye.

The clue is in the small wordsOne dot per paper: how often it uses the word, per 1,000 words

Words like "upon" and "by" carry no topic, so a writer uses them out of habit. That makes them a fingerprint. Mosteller and Wallace used 70 of them; so do we.

Part 2 of 5

Each author is a die

How would each of them write a paper?

A story of how a paper gets writtenA generative model: a recipe that could have produced the data

1. Pick the author: Madison with probability \(\phi_y\), Hamilton otherwise.

2. Each author owns a 70-sided die, one face per function word. Roll that author's die once for every function word in the paper.

That is the whole model. It ignores word order and meaning, which is naive, and it is enough.

Notation, used all lecture
\(y\)the author: 1 for Madison, 0 for Hamilton
\(k\)one of the 70 function words
\(x_t\)the \(t\)-th function word of a paper, \(t = 1, \dots, L\)
\(\phi_y\)\(P(y = 1)\): how likely a paper is Madison's
\(\phi_{k \mid y}\)\(P(x_t = k \mid y)\): the face \(k\) of author \(y\)'s die

The two dice, learned from 65 papersEach face's probability is a count divided by a total

Why counting is the best estimateMaximum likelihood for one die

Hamilton's papers contain \(N\) function words, \(n_k\) of them word \(k\). The probability his die gives to all of them is

\( L(\phi) = \prod_{k=1}^{70} \phi_{k \mid 0}^{\,n_k}, \qquad \sum_k \phi_{k \mid 0} = 1 \)

Take the log, \( \sum_k n_k \log \phi_{k \mid 0} \), and maximize it while the faces sum to one (a Lagrange multiplier):

\( \phi_{k \mid 0} = \dfrac{n_k}{N} \)
In words

The best die is the one whose faces match how often he actually used each word. Training is counting.

For \(\phi_y\), the same argument gives the share of training papers that are Madison's: \( 14 / 65 \).

The coin of the maximum likelihood lecture, with 70 faces instead of two.

Part 3 of 5

Run the story backwards

Given a paper, which die rolled it?

Bayes' rule turns the story around

\( \log \dfrac{P(y = 1 \mid x)}{P(y = 0 \mid x)} \;=\; \log \dfrac{\phi_y}{1 - \phi_y} \;+\; \sum_{t=1}^{L} \log \dfrac{\phi_{x_t \mid 1}}{\phi_{x_t \mid 0}} \)
Read it as a voteStart from the prior. Every function word in the paper adds its own vote: positive if Madison's die favours it, negative if Hamilton's does. Above zero, Madison.
Why a sumThe model rolls the die independently for each word, so the probability of the paper is a product, and its log is a sum. The derivation is the next deep dive.

From Bayes' rule to a sum of votes

1. Bayes for each author: \( P(y \mid x) = P(x \mid y)\, P(y) / P(x) \).

2. Divide Madison by Hamilton: \(P(x)\) cancels.

\( \dfrac{P(y = 1 \mid x)}{P(y = 0 \mid x)} = \dfrac{\phi_y}{1 - \phi_y} \cdot \dfrac{P(x \mid y = 1)}{P(x \mid y = 0)} \)

3. The die is rolled independently: \( P(x \mid y) = \prod_{t=1}^{L} \phi_{x_t \mid y} \). This is the naive assumption.

4. Take the log: products become sums, one vote per word.

5. Back to a probability: \( P(y = 1 \mid x) = 1 / (1 + e^{-\text{log odds}}) \), the sigmoid. A word used \(c\) times votes \(c\) times, so the log odds is linear in the word counts: Naive Bayes draws the same kind of boundary as logistic regression.

Weigh a paperThe twelve largest votes, and the verdict. Pick any paper.

"upon" can only vote for Hamilton: Madison used it in 3 of his 14 papers. Ten of the twelve disputed papers never use it; No. 50 uses it once, No. 54 twice.

The word a die has never rolledTrain on only three papers per author, and a zero appears

smoothing \(\alpha\)

\( \phi_{k \mid y} = \dfrac{n_{k \mid y} + \alpha}{N_y + 70\,\alpha} \)

\(n_{k \mid y}\): how often author \(y\) used word \(k\) in training; \(N_y\): all their function words. Adding \(\alpha\) pretend sightings of every face turns "never seen, so impossible" into "never seen, so rare".

The verdict on the twelveTrained on all 65 known papers, with \(\alpha = 1\). Positive: Madison.

Checked firstLeaving each of the 65 known papers out in turn and predicting it, the model is right every time.
The hard oneNo. 55 leans Hamilton here. Mosteller and Wallace also found it the weakest case: still Madison, but at odds of only a few hundred to one, far weaker than for the others. A model that hesitates where historians hesitate is telling you something true.

Part 4 of 5

Two numbers instead of seventy

What if the features are continuous?

Gaussian discriminant analysisEach paper as two rates. Each author as a cloud: a 2D Gaussian.

across   up

\( y \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\phi_y) \)
\( r \mid y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_y, \Sigma) \)

\(r\): the paper's two rates, per 1,000 words. \(\mu_y\): author \(y\)'s average. \(\Sigma\): the spread. Fitting is averaging, the continuous version of counting.

Where the Gaussian is wrongA model that is wrong about the data, and still right about the author

GDA still separates the authors, because the two clouds are far apart. A generative model makes a strong claim about how the data was made; when the claim is wrong, its probabilities are off even when its decisions are right. Pick "on" against "by" on the last slide: closer to Gaussian, and the clouds overlap more.

A shared \(\Sigma\) gives a straight boundary

The log odds is the difference of two Gaussian log densities plus the prior:

\( \log \dfrac{P(y = 1 \mid r)}{P(y = 0 \mid r)} = -\tfrac{1}{2}(r - \mu_1)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (r - \mu_1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(r - \mu_0)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (r - \mu_0) + \log \dfrac{\phi_y}{1 - \phi_y} \)

Expand: the \( r^\top \Sigma^{-1} r \) terms are the same for both authors and cancel. What is left is linear in \(r\):

\( \log \dfrac{P(y = 1 \mid r)}{P(y = 0 \mid r)} = \theta^\top r + \theta_0, \qquad \theta = \Sigma^{-1} (\mu_1 - \mu_0) \)

So \( P(y = 1 \mid r) = \sigma(\theta^\top r + \theta_0) \): exactly the form of logistic regression. With one \(\Sigma\) per author the quadratic terms stay, and the boundary curves.

Part 5 of 5

Generative or discriminative?

Which learns faster from few examples?

Two ways to learn the same boundary

Generative: Naive Bayes, GDA

Model how each author writes, \(P(x \mid y)\) and \(P(y)\), then flip it with Bayes' rule. Strong assumptions. Fitting is counting and averaging.

Discriminative: logistic regression

Model only the answer, \(P(y \mid x)\), and fit the boundary directly by gradient descent. Weaker assumptions. Never says how a paper is written.

Both end in a sigmoid of a linear score. The question is which finds a good one from less data.

Learning from a handful of papersTrain on a few papers per author, test on all the other known ones; 60 random draws per point

With very few papers, the model that assumes more learns faster: its assumptions fill in what the data cannot. With enough data the discriminative model catches up, and where the assumptions are wrong it eventually wins. Ng and Jordan (2002) proved this trade-off.

Mosteller and Wallace in twenty linesNaive Bayes from scratch, on the same counts. Press Run.


import numpy as np, pandas as pd

d = pd.read_csv('federalist_function_words.csv')
words = d.columns[3:]                                   # the 70 function words
known = d[d.author.isin(['Hamilton', 'Madison'])]

def fit(train, alpha=1):
    phi = {}
    for a in ['Hamilton', 'Madison']:
        c = train[train.author == a][words].sum() + alpha    # how often a wrote each word, plus alpha
        phi[a] = c / c.sum()                                  # the author's die
    return phi, (train.author == 'Madison').mean()

def log_odds(paper, phi, prior):                        # above 0 means Madison
    return np.log(prior / (1 - prior)) + (paper[words] * np.log(phi['Madison'] / phi['Hamilton'])).sum()

hits = 0
for i, p in known.iterrows():                           # leave one paper out, predict it
    phi, prior = fit(known.drop(i))
    hits += (log_odds(p, phi, prior) > 0) == (p.author == 'Madison')
print('leave-one-out accuracy:', round(hits / len(known), 3))

phi, prior = fit(known)
for _, p in d[d.author == 'disputed'].iterrows():
    print(f'No. {p.paper}: log odds for Madison {log_odds(p, phi, prior):+.1f}')

You should see a leave-one-out accuracy of 1.0, and eleven positive log odds: every disputed paper but No. 55 goes to Madison.

What to take away

  1. A generative model is a story of how the data was made: here, each author rolls a die of function words.
  2. Fitting it is counting (or averaging): maximum likelihood gives the observed frequencies. Smoothing keeps an unseen word from vetoing everything.
  3. Classifying is running the story backwards with Bayes' rule; the log odds is a sum of one vote per word.
  4. GDA does the same with Gaussian clouds; with a shared covariance its boundary is exactly a logistic regression.
  5. Strong assumptions learn fast from little data, and are wrong in ways you should check. On the twelve disputed papers, 65 known ones were enough to agree with Mosteller and Wallace.
